\chapter*{Abstract}
\markboth{Abstract}{Abstract}

Modern language models expose many representations besides their final text:
embeddings, hidden states, logits, residual streams, and other latent signals
are now used for retrieval, interpretability, inversion, and automatic
evaluation. A central question is whether methods that map these
representations back to natural language preserve the semantic content of the
original input, or whether they merely produce fluent and plausible text. This
distinction matters especially for inputs involving negation, logical polarity,
counterfactual statements, or violations of commonsense, where a reconstruction
can remain lexically similar while changing the meaning.

This thesis studies semantic fidelity in latent-to-text methods and evaluates
whether activation-derived evidence can improve prompt optimization for
LLM-as-a-judge evaluation. The work combines dataset construction, diagnostic
experiments on inversion and verbalization methods, and a G-EVAL-style
evaluation pipeline optimized with GEPA. The method can be summarized as a
two-track analysis: first, latent representations are inverted or verbalized
and checked for semantic preservation; second, perplexity, Natural Language
Activation verbalizations, and auxiliary-judge feedback are tested as signals
for improving judge prompts.

The experimental setup uses semantic-stress examples, SIPIT-style hidden-state
inversion diagnostics, NLA activation verbalizations from a base language
model, and G-EVAL-style datasets evaluated with correlation metrics against
human judgments. Early results show that perplexity feedback can improve GEPA
prompt optimization in this setting, while raw NLA feedback is not yet a
reliably positive signal without further transformation. These findings frame
the thesis as both an empirical study of semantic fidelity and a diagnostic
analysis of how activation information can be made useful for downstream
evaluation.

\chapter*{Introduction}
\markboth{Introduction}{Introduction}

Large Language Models (LLMs) are usually observed through the text they
generate, but their behavior is mediated by internal numerical representations.
Embeddings, hidden states, logits, residual streams, and soft prompts encode
information that can be reused for retrieval, analysis, generation, or
evaluation. As a result, a growing line of work studies whether these latent
representations can be inverted or verbalized: given a vector or an internal
state, can we recover what the model was representing?

This question is often evaluated as a reconstruction problem. If the recovered
text overlaps with the input, has high embedding similarity, or receives a high
semantic-similarity score, the method appears successful. However, this view can
hide a more delicate failure mode. A reconstruction may look close while
removing a negation, flipping a causal relation, or replacing a counterfactual
statement with a more plausible commonsense sentence. For example, the sentence
\quotes{The ice does not melt under the sun} and the sentence \quotes{The ice
melts under the sun} are lexically close, but their meanings are incompatible.
Surface-level metrics can treat them as similar even though the logical content
has changed.

The goal of this thesis is to study this mismatch between surface-form
reconstruction and semantic fidelity. The focus is not only whether a latent
representation can be decoded into fluent text, but whether the recovered text
preserves meaning in cases where meaning is fragile: negation, polarity,
contradiction, counterfactuality, and commonsense violations. These phenomena
are important because language models are strongly biased toward plausible
continuations. If an inversion method normalizes an unusual input into a more
likely sentence, it may look successful by standard metrics while failing the
semantic property that matters.

The work is organized around two connected experimental tracks. The first track
concerns latent-to-text inversion and verbalization. It includes embedding
inversion diagnostics, SIPIT-style hidden-state prompt recovery, and Natural
Language Activation (NLA) verbalizations. SIPIT is relevant because it treats
hidden-state inversion as prompt recovery, while NLA is relevant because it maps
internal activations to readable descriptions rather than necessarily trying to
reconstruct the original prompt exactly. Together, these methods offer different
ways to ask what information is present in a representation and how faithfully
that information can be expressed in text.

The second track uses G-EVAL-style LLM-as-a-judge tasks as a downstream testbed.
In this setting, a language model scores generated text according to a rubric,
and the quality of the judge is measured by agreement with human annotations.
The thesis uses GEPA, a prompt optimization method, to improve judge prompts and
then studies whether additional signals can help the optimization process.
Perplexity is used as a model-confidence signal computed from the base model.
NLA verbalizations are tested as activation-derived feedback. An optional
auxiliary judge can also be used to compress raw feedback into a form that is
closer to the evaluation rubric.

This framing leads to the following research questions. First, do
embedding-inversion and activation-inversion methods preserve logical semantic
content, or do they reconstruct more plausible but altered text? Second, do
standard reconstruction metrics capture errors such as lost negation, inverted
polarity, or commonsense normalization? Third, can SIPIT recover prompts under
standard and logical stress-test settings, and what changes when continuous
random prefixes are introduced? Fourth, do NLA verbalizations preserve
semantically important activation content, or do they produce generic
completion-like descriptions? Fifth, can GEPA improve LLM-as-a-judge prompts on
G-EVAL-style tasks, and does adding perplexity, NLA, or auxiliary-judge feedback
make those improvements more reliable?

The thesis does not aim to claim that every tested method is improved by NLA in
its raw form. Instead, it treats negative results as part of the analysis. If
raw activation verbalizations do not improve prompt optimization, the relevant
question becomes why: whether the selected token positions are uninformative,
whether the verbalizations are too generic, whether the proposer model cannot
use them, or whether the signal must first be transformed into rubric-level
feedback. This diagnostic perspective is important because the broader research
problem is not only to use latent information, but to understand when latent
information is aligned with the downstream task.

Our thesis work is subdivided into the following structure:

\chapter{Theoretical Framework}
\label{chapter:theory}

\section{Neural Text Representations}

\subsection{From Tokens To Internal States}

Language models process text through numerical representations. Tokens are
mapped to vectors, vectors are transformed layer after layer, and the final
model output is produced from a distribution over the vocabulary. This thesis
uses several representation types, so it is useful to distinguish their roles
before discussing inversion and evaluation.

\textbf{Text embeddings} are fixed-size vectors associated with words,
sentences, passages, or documents. They are commonly used for retrieval and
semantic similarity because nearby vectors are expected to represent similar
content. In this thesis, embeddings are relevant both as objects that can be
inverted and as examples of representations whose similarity may not be
sufficient to guarantee semantic fidelity.

\textbf{Hidden states} are the internal vectors produced by a language model at
each layer and token position. In a decoder-only model, the hidden state of a
token is contextual: it depends on the previous tokens and on the transformations
performed by the network. Hidden states are therefore richer than static token
embeddings. They can contain information about the prompt, the next-token
prediction task, and intermediate features used by the model.

\textbf{Residual streams} are the running internal representations to which
attention and feed-forward blocks add their contributions. They are especially
important in mechanistic interpretability because they provide a common space
where different components of the Transformer write information. This makes
them natural targets for methods that try to \textbf{inspect}, \textbf{describe},
or \textbf{decode} what a model represents internally.

\subsection{Output-Side And Prompt-Side Representations}

\textbf{Logits} are the pre-softmax scores assigned to each vocabulary token
before the model samples or selects an output token. After applying a softmax,
the logits become a probability distribution. Logits are useful because they
expose not only the selected token, but also the alternatives the model
considered likely.

\textbf{Soft prompts} are continuous prompt-like vectors optimized directly in
embedding space. Unlike ordinary prompts, they are not necessarily readable as
text. Their existence highlights a central issue for this thesis: a
representation can be useful to a model while remaining difficult to interpret
for humans.

\section{Latent-To-Text Inversion}

\subsection{The General Recovery Question}

Latent-to-text inversion asks what can be recovered from a latent
representation. The input may be an embedding, a hidden state, logits, or another
internal activation; the output is text that attempts to reconstruct or describe
the information contained in that representation. This broad framing includes
several related but distinct tasks.

\subsection{Main Inversion Targets}

In \textbf{embedding inversion}, the input is usually a sentence or document
embedding. The goal is to recover the original text, or at least a close
semantic approximation. This task matters because embeddings are often treated
as compressed semantic summaries. If sensitive or detailed text can be recovered
from embeddings, then embeddings may leak more information than expected.

In \textbf{hidden-state inversion}, the input is an internal state of a language
model. The target is typically the prompt or prefix that produced that state.
This task is more model-specific because hidden states depend on the architecture
and layer where they are extracted. It is also more direct for decoder-only
models, since the hidden state is part of the causal computation that produces
the next token.

In \textbf{activation verbalization}, the output is not necessarily the original
text. Here, an \textbf{activation} is an intermediate numerical state produced
inside a model while it processes an input, such as a hidden vector at a
specific layer and token position. To \textbf{verbalize} an activation means to
translate that internal state into a natural-language description of what it
appears to encode. A verbalization may say that an activation concerns a
refusal, a factual correction, a sentiment, or a mathematical operation without
reconstructing the exact input sentence.

\subsection{Different Notions Of Success}

These tasks should not be collapsed into a single notion of success. Exact
reconstruction, plausible paraphrase, semantic preservation, and useful
description are different targets. The rest of the thesis uses this distinction
to avoid overclaiming: a method can be useful while still failing to preserve
the specific semantic property being tested.

\section{Semantic Fidelity}

\subsection{Surface Similarity Is Not Meaning}

Semantic fidelity is the degree to which an output preserves the meaning of the
input, not merely its surface form. A reconstruction can share many words with
the original and still be semantically wrong. This is especially clear when the
input contains small linguistic markers with large logical effects.

Negation is the simplest example. The sentences \quotes{The treatment did not
improve the symptoms} and \quotes{The treatment improved the symptoms} differ
by one word, but they support opposite conclusions. A metric based on lexical
overlap may assign them a high score, even though the reconstruction has lost
the most important information.

\subsection{Logical Stress Cases}

Logical polarity and causal direction create similar problems. If an input says
that an event prevents an outcome, while the reconstruction says that the event
causes the outcome, the semantic relation has been inverted. Counterfactuals and
commonsense violations are another stress case. Because language models are
trained to produce likely text, they may normalize unusual statements toward
more plausible ones. A method that reconstructs \quotes{The glass did not break
after falling on concrete} as \quotes{The glass broke after falling on concrete}
has produced a more typical sentence, but not a faithful one.

\subsection{Consequences For Evaluation}

This thesis therefore treats standard reconstruction metrics as incomplete.
Token overlap, semantic similarity, and embedding similarity remain useful, but
they must be complemented by targeted checks for meaning-preserving behavior.
The central empirical question is whether inversion and verbalization methods
preserve the aspects of meaning that are easiest to lose.

\section{Exact Hidden-State Recovery}

\subsection{Input Recovery As A Stronger Target}

Exact hidden-state recovery asks whether an internal state contains enough
information to identify the input text that produced it. This is a stronger
target than ordinary language generation. In generation, a model may produce a
sentence that is plausible after a context; in recovery, the objective is to
infer the original context itself from the representation.

This distinction matters because plausible text is not necessarily faithful
text. If an internal state came from the sentence \quotes{The treatment did not
improve the symptoms}, a continuation model only needs to produce likely text
given that context. A recovery method instead tries to recover the input
sentence or an equivalent representation of it. The second target gives a much
stricter way to ask what information is actually present in the hidden state.

\subsection{Why It Matters For Semantic Fidelity}

Exact recovery is useful as a conceptual upper bound for semantic fidelity. If a
method recovers the original input exactly, then negation, polarity, and
counterfactual content are preserved by construction. If it only returns a
plausible paraphrase, then semantic fidelity must be evaluated explicitly,
because fluency can hide meaning changes.

For this reason, hidden-state recovery helps separate two questions that are
otherwise easy to confuse: \textbf{can a model produce reasonable text?} and
\textbf{does the representation preserve the original meaning?} The later
method and related-work chapters introduce specific systems and experimental
controls; here, the relevant theoretical point is only the distinction between
recovering an input and generating a likely text.

\section{Activation Verbalization}

\subsection{What Is Being Verbalized}

An activation is a model-internal numerical object, not a sentence. It may be a
single vector at one layer and token position, a residual-stream state, or a
small collection of internal states selected around a model decision. Since
these objects are not directly readable, activation verbalization tries to map
them into ordinary language.

The word \textbf{verbalization} is used here in a narrow sense: it does not mean
that the activation is converted back into the exact prompt. It means that a
model or procedure produces a textual description of the information that the
activation seems to carry.

\subsection{Description Rather Than Reconstruction}

Activation verbalization provides a different view of latent-to-text analysis.
Instead of treating activations only as vectors to be decoded into the original
prompt, it asks whether an activation can be translated into a readable natural
language description. The output is therefore a \textbf{description of internal
content}, not necessarily a reconstruction of the exact input string.

This idea is attractive because it can make internal model states inspectable.
If an activation carries information about negation, contradiction, uncertainty,
or task-specific reasoning, a verbalization might expose that information in a
form that can be read by a human or reused by another model. In this sense,
activation verbalization sits between interpretability and inversion: it does
not promise exact decoding, but it can provide a semantic hypothesis about what
the model is representing.

\subsection{Faithfulness Caveats}

However, a verbalization is not automatically faithful. It may be generic,
completion-like, or focused on a token-level feature that is not useful for the
task. The choice of token position, layer, and verbalization strategy matters.
For example, verbalizing only the final token may give less semantic context
than verbalizing several candidate tokens around a model decision. Later
chapters use these theoretical distinctions to interpret method-specific
results without assuming that every readable description is also a faithful one.

\section{Perplexity And Model Confidence}

\subsection{Definition}

Perplexity is a standard way to express how surprising a sequence is under a
language model. If a model assigns high probability to the observed tokens, the
perplexity is low. If the model assigns low probability, the perplexity is high.
In simplified terms, perplexity can be read as the effective number of choices
the model considered at each step.

For an output sequence $y_1, \ldots, y_n$ conditioned on a context $x$, the
token-level negative log-likelihood is
\[
    -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(y_i \mid x, y_{<i}).
\]
Perplexity is the exponential of this value:
\[
    \mathrm{PPL}(y \mid x) =
    \exp\left(-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(y_i \mid x, y_{<i})\right).
\]

\subsection{Use As An Auxiliary Signal}

In this thesis, perplexity is used as auxiliary feedback for prompt
optimization. It is computed from the evaluated model and can indicate whether a
generated score or explanation was natural under that model. This signal is not
the same as correctness and it is not the same as agreement with human
annotations. A low-perplexity answer can still be wrong. Nevertheless, it can
provide useful evidence about model confidence or hesitation when combined with
task metrics.

\section{LLM-As-A-Judge Evaluation}

\subsection{Rubrics And Scoring Tasks}

A \textbf{rubric} is a written set of criteria that defines how an output should
be evaluated. For example, a rubric may ask whether an answer is factually
consistent with a source document, or whether a summary covers the important
information from an input. The rubric defines the \textbf{evaluation task}: what
object is being judged, which criterion matters, and what kind of score should
be produced.

\subsection{The LLM As The Scoring Procedure}

LLM-as-a-judge evaluation is a method for carrying out such a scoring task. A
language model receives the material to evaluate, the rubric, and usually an
instruction specifying the output format. It then produces a score, sometimes
with a short explanation. In this setting, the LLM is not the task itself; it is
the \textbf{scoring procedure} used to approximate or automate a judgment that
would otherwise be produced by humans.

This distinction avoids an important ambiguity. A dataset or benchmark can
define examples, human annotations, and target scores. A judge prompt can define
how the model should apply a rubric. The LLM-as-a-judge method is the act of
using the model to assign scores under those instructions. Later chapters
introduce the specific benchmarks and implementations used in this thesis.

\subsection{The Judge Prompt As Part Of The Measurement}

The prompt given to the judge is part of the measurement procedure. It translates
the rubric into operational instructions for the model: what to read, what to
ignore, how to reason about the criterion, and how to format the score. If the
prompt is underspecified, the model may use irrelevant cues. If it is too rigid,
it may ignore context that a human evaluator would consider. This makes
LLM-as-a-judge evaluation both a measurement problem and a prompt-design
problem.

\section{Agreement Metrics}

\subsection{Agreement Rather Than Absolute Truth}

When a judge model assigns scores to examples that already have human
annotations, performance is measured by agreement between the model scores and
the human scores. The most important distinction is that these metrics evaluate
relative alignment, not absolute truth. A judge is useful if examples that
humans rate higher also tend to receive higher model scores.

\subsection{Correlation Metrics}

Pearson correlation measures linear association between two sets of scores. It
is sensitive to the scale of the values and answers the question of whether
model scores increase proportionally with human scores. Spearman correlation
uses ranks instead of raw values, so it measures whether the order induced by
the model agrees with the order induced by humans. Kendall correlation also
focuses on rank agreement and can be interpreted through concordant and
discordant pairs.

\subsection{Interpreting Improvements}

These metrics have different sensitivities, so a complete evaluation should
report the paper-aligned set rather than selecting only the metric that improves
most. Later chapters define the exact metric boundaries, datasets, dimensions,
and comparison protocol used in the experiments. For Chapter 1, the key idea is
that agreement metrics determine whether a judge prompt actually behaves more
like human annotators.

\section{Prompt Optimization}

\subsection{Prompts As Search Objects}

Prompt optimization is the process of improving a prompt so that a language
model performs better on a target task. A prompt can define the task, specify a
rubric, constrain the output format, provide examples, or warn the model about
common errors. Because LLM behavior is highly prompt-sensitive, manual prompt
engineering is often insufficient for systematic comparison.

Automatic prompt optimization treats prompt design as an iterative search
problem. A candidate prompt is evaluated on a set of examples. The failures,
scores, and other available feedback can then be used to suggest a revised
prompt. The revised prompt is evaluated again, and better candidates are kept
according to validation performance.

\subsection{Optimization In Judge Tasks}

In judge tasks, prompt optimization operates on the instructions given to the
judge model. The evaluation task and the human reference scores stay fixed; what
changes is the textual interface that tells the model how to apply the rubric.
This boundary is important: optimizing the prompt does not redefine the task,
but it can change how reliably the model performs the task.

Over time, the search can produce prompts that are \textbf{longer},
\textbf{more explicit}, and \textbf{more aligned} with the target evaluation
metric. The method-specific optimizer used later in the thesis is introduced in
the related-work chapter and then described operationally in the method chapter.

This chapter introduced the theoretical concepts needed by the rest of the
thesis. The next chapter positions these concepts within previous work on
embedding inversion, hidden-state inversion, activation verbalization,
interpretable prompts, LLM-as-a-judge evaluation, and prompt optimization.

\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chapter:related-work}

This chapter positions the thesis with respect to the main research lines that
it builds on. The goal is not to provide a general survey of language models,
but to identify the precise gaps that motivate the experimental work. Previous
work has studied text inversion, hidden-state recovery, activation
interpretability, LLM-based evaluation, and prompt optimization mostly as
separate problems. This thesis connects them through a single question:
\textbf{can latent evidence be turned into useful natural-language guidance
without losing the semantic distinctions that matter for evaluation?}

\section{Embedding Inversion}
\label{sec:related-embedding-inversion}

Embedding inversion studies whether a text embedding contains enough
information to recover the text that produced it. The problem is important both
for privacy and for interpretability. If a sentence embedding can be inverted
into a near-exact sentence, then the representation stores more surface and
semantic information than its fixed dimensionality may suggest.

Early generative attacks showed that sentence embeddings can leak substantial
information about the original input. GEIA, for instance, frames inversion as a
generation problem and demonstrates that embeddings can support recovery of
whole sentences rather than only coarse semantic summaries
\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2305-03010}. Later work moves toward more general and less
task-specific settings. Universal zero-shot inversion aims to recover text from
embeddings without training a separate attacker for each embedding model
\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2504-00147}. Zero2Text similarly studies cross-domain inversion
without a dedicated training phase for the target domain \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2602-01757}.
Conditional masked diffusion methods, such as the Jina embedding inversion
work, approach the problem through iterative denoising conditioned on the
observed embedding \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2602-11047}.

These works establish that embeddings are not opaque summaries. They can retain
recoverable lexical, syntactic, and semantic information. However, the main
success criteria are usually reconstruction quality, token overlap, semantic
similarity, or attack success. This thesis uses that line of work as the
starting point, but asks a narrower question: \textbf{does the recovered text
preserve logically fragile meaning?} A reconstruction that removes a negation
or normalizes a counterfactual statement may still look strong under standard
similarity metrics. The contribution here is therefore not a new embedding
inversion attack, but a semantic-fidelity analysis of what inversion-style
outputs preserve and what they distort.

\section{Hidden-State Inversion And SIPIT}
\label{sec:related-sipit}

Hidden-state inversion is closely related to embedding inversion, but the
representation being inverted is produced inside a decoder-only language model.
Instead of starting from an external sentence embedding, the inversion method
starts from hidden states generated by the model while processing a prompt.
This makes the task more directly tied to autoregressive LLMs and to the
information carried through their internal computation.

SIPIT is the central reference for this part of the thesis. It argues that
language models can be injective under the considered setting and therefore
invertible, and it provides a practical method for reconstructing prompts from
hidden states \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2510-15511}. This is a stronger target than
producing a plausible paraphrase: the method is evaluated by whether it can
recover the prompt that generated the state. SIPIT is therefore useful as a
methodological anchor for the distinction introduced in Chapter
\ref{chapter:theory}: \textbf{recovering the input} is not the same as
\textbf{continuing from the input}.

The thesis uses SIPIT in two roles. First, it provides a concrete SOTA
hidden-state inversion baseline for prompt recovery. Second, it motivates
diagnostics where exact recovery is compared with semantic preservation under
harder logical or continuous-prefix settings. The gap relative to SIPIT is that
this work is not satisfied with exactness only on standard prompts. It asks
whether the inversion behavior remains informative when the original input
contains negation, contradiction, or other meaning-sensitive phenomena.

\section{Semantic Stress Tests And Negation}
\label{sec:related-semantic-stress-tests}

The importance of negation and logical polarity has been studied in benchmark
construction for language understanding. \textit{This is not a Dataset}
collects and organizes negation examples to challenge LLMs in settings where a
single negation marker can change the truth conditions of a sentence
\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2310-15941}. This type of benchmark is relevant because
standard semantic similarity can be misleading: two sentences may be close in
form while expressing incompatible meanings.

For this thesis, semantic stress tests provide the bridge between inversion and
evaluation. Inversion methods are often judged by whether the output is fluent
and close to the original. But if the input is \quotes{The treatment did not
improve the symptoms} and the reconstruction is \quotes{The treatment improved
the symptoms}, the reconstruction has failed the property that matters most,
even if many tokens are shared. Negation, polarity, counterfactuality, and
commonsense violations therefore act as \textbf{high-sensitivity probes}: they
make meaning changes visible in a way that aggregate similarity scores may not.

This thesis adapts that idea to latent-to-text analysis. The semantic-stress
examples are not introduced as another general natural-language understanding
benchmark. They are used to test whether latent representations and their
verbalizations preserve the meaning components that are easiest to normalize
away.

\section{Prompt Waywardness And Soft-Prompt Interpretability}
\label{sec:related-soft-prompts}

Soft prompts are continuous vectors optimized to control a model without being
ordinary readable text. They are effective, but their interpretability is
problematic because the nearest vocabulary tokens to a learned vector need not
represent what the prompt is doing.

Prompt waywardness formalizes this issue: continuous prompts can behave in ways
that are not faithfully explained by mapping them to nearby discrete tokens
\cite{DBLP:conf/naacl/KhashabiLMQ0WHK22}. This matters because it warns against a
naive interpretation strategy. If a vector is close to a word embedding, that
word is not necessarily a correct explanation of the vector's functional role.
More recent work on interpretable soft prompts studies methods for making
learned prompt vectors more understandable while preserving task performance
\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2504-02144}.

This line of work is relevant to two parts of the thesis. In the SIPIT branch,
continuous or random prefix experiments test whether inversion methods remain
meaningful when the input representation is not a simple natural-language
prompt. In the later soft-prompt branch, the same concern motivates training
task-specific soft prompts and then asking whether inversion or verbalization
methods can explain the learned soft embeddings. The thesis therefore treats
soft prompts as an interpretability stress case: useful internal vectors may be
hard to verbalize faithfully.

\section{Natural Language Activations}
\label{sec:related-nla}

Natural Language Activations (NLA) provide a different way to inspect model
internals.\footnote{\href{https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/nla/index.html\#introduction}{NLA publication on the Transformer Circuits website}.}
Instead of directly reconstructing the original prompt, NLA maps an activation
vector to a natural-language description and can also reconstruct an activation
from such a description \cite{frasertaliente2026nla}. The component used in
this thesis is the activation verbalizer, because the relevant direction is
latent-to-text: an internal state is turned into a readable description.

NLA is important for this thesis because it offers a potential interface
between \textbf{model internals} and \textbf{prompt optimization}. If an
activation verbalization describes what the model is uncertain about, what
semantic property it is tracking, or which part of the input drives a decision,
then that text may be useful as feedback for another model. This is the
intuition behind the GEPA experiments with NLA: activation-derived text is
given to the prompt proposer as additional evidence.

At the same time, NLA introduces new experimental choices. The verbalized
activation depends on the selected model, layer, token position, and token
window. A verbalization can be semantically useful, but it can also be generic,
too local, or focused on completion behavior rather than on the rubric used by
the downstream task. This thesis therefore does not assume that raw NLA
descriptions are automatically beneficial. It evaluates whether they become
useful feedback, and when they do not, it treats the failure as evidence about
the mismatch between activation-level descriptions and task-level guidance.

\section{LLM-As-A-Judge Evaluation And G-EVAL}
\label{sec:related-geval}

LLM-as-a-judge evaluation uses a language model to assign scores to generated
text according to a rubric. The promise is practical: human evaluation is
expensive, while an LLM judge can score many examples consistently under a
fixed prompt. The risk is methodological: the judge prompt, the model family,
and the output parsing procedure become part of the measurement instrument.

G-EVAL is the main reference for the evaluation branch of this thesis. It uses
GPT-4 as an evaluator for natural language generation tasks and reports stronger
alignment with human judgments than several previous automatic metrics
\cite{DBLP:conf/emnlp/LiuIXWXZ23}. Its setup is especially relevant because it defines
rubric-based evaluation dimensions and reports correlations with human scores.
Those correlation metrics, rather than the raw model scores alone, are the
scientific target for comparison.

This thesis does not treat G-EVAL as synonymous with LLM-as-a-judge evaluation.
G-EVAL is a particular method and experimental protocol within the broader
LLM-as-a-judge family. The thesis adopts G-EVAL-style datasets, dimensions, and
agreement metrics as the benchmark surface, while using a different local model
and a prompt-optimization pipeline. This distinction is important for
interpretation: improvements in this thesis evaluate whether the optimized
judge prompt aligns better with human scores in a G-EVAL-style setting, not
whether the original GPT-4 G-EVAL system has been exactly reproduced.

\section{Prompt Optimization And GEPA}
\label{sec:related-gepa}

Prompt optimization methods improve a model's behavior by changing the prompt
rather than updating model weights. In judge tasks, this means that the rubric,
input fields, output format, and common-error warnings can be rewritten while
the underlying examples and human labels remain fixed.

GEPA is the prompt optimizer used in this thesis. It performs reflective prompt
evolution: candidate prompts are evaluated, failures are summarized in natural
language, and a proposer model generates prompt revisions that are tested on
subsets and validation examples \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2507-19457}. GEPA is particularly
appropriate for this work because its optimization loop is itself language
mediated. It can incorporate textual feedback, not only scalar rewards. That
makes it a natural place to test perplexity summaries, NLA verbalizations, and
auxiliary-judge comments as additional proposer context.

The thesis differs from the GEPA paper in the role assigned to the optimizer.
The goal is not to benchmark GEPA broadly against reinforcement learning or
other prompt optimizers. Instead, GEPA is used as an experimental mechanism for
asking whether latent-derived feedback helps a prompt proposer discover better
LLM-as-a-judge prompts. The prompt trajectory is therefore as important as the
final score: intermediate prompts can reveal whether feedback changes the
optimizer's reasoning or only adds noise. Chapter \ref{chapter:theory}
introduced perplexity as a model surprisal signal, while this chapter introduced
NLA as an activation-verbalization method. The method chapter combines these
signals with GEPA, treating them as \textbf{proposal aids} rather than as final
evaluation metrics.

\section{Summary Of The Research Gap}
\label{sec:related-summary-gap}

The reviewed work provides the building blocks for this thesis but leaves an
open integration problem. Embedding and hidden-state inversion show that latent
representations can contain recoverable information. Semantic stress tests show
that standard similarity can miss meaning-changing errors. Soft-prompt work
shows that useful continuous vectors are not automatically interpretable. NLA
offers a way to verbalize activations, and G-EVAL-style tasks provide a
downstream setting where judge quality can be measured against human
annotations. GEPA supplies the optimization loop that can consume textual
feedback. What remains underexplored is whether latent-derived evidence, once
expressed as text, is useful enough to guide that optimization loop.

The thesis contribution is to connect these pieces experimentally. It asks
whether latent-derived textual evidence, especially perplexity and NLA
verbalizations, can improve prompt optimization for LLM-as-a-judge evaluation,
and whether the same methods preserve semantic fidelity on logically fragile
inputs. This combined perspective is the gap addressed by the method and
experimental chapters that follow.

\chapter{Method}
\label{chapter:method}

This chapter describes the method used in the thesis. The goal is not yet to
report final numerical results or cluster-level implementation details. Instead,
the chapter defines the experimental objects, the information flow, and the
method variants that are later instantiated in Chapter~4 and evaluated in
Chapter~5.

The method has two connected parts. The first part studies whether latent
representations can be mapped back to text while preserving meaning. It includes
embedding-inversion diagnostics, SIPIT-style hidden-state inversion, and Natural
Language Activation verbalization. The second part uses a G-EVAL-style
LLM-as-a-judge task as a downstream setting where activation-derived signals can
be tested as feedback for prompt optimization. GEPA is therefore not the whole
thesis method; it is the branch where latent-derived evidence is tested as a
practical signal for improving judge prompts.

\section{Canonical Semantic-Fidelity Dataset}
\label{sec:method-canonical-dataset}

The thesis uses a canonical semantic-fidelity dataset to stress latent-to-text
methods. The dataset is not intended to be a large benchmark by itself. Its role
is to provide controlled examples where a reconstruction can look plausible
while changing the logical meaning of the input.

Each row is represented with stable metadata. At minimum, a row contains a
stable identifier, a source block, a phenomenon label, an input sentence, an
optional paired sentence, and split metadata. The paired sentence is useful when
the phenomenon is defined by a contrast, such as a negated sentence and its
non-negated counterpart. Stable identifiers make it possible to join outputs
from different branches: SIPIT recovery, NLA verbalization, embedding-inversion
diagnostics, and later qualitative analysis.

to the property under investigation.

The dataset is used as a common reference object across branches. In the SIPIT
branch, rows can be tokenized into fixed prompt windows. In the NLA branch, the
same rows define which activation positions should be verbalized. In the
embedding-inversion branch, they provide diagnostic inputs for observing whether
reconstructions preserve logical content.

\section{Embedding-Inversion Diagnostic Branch}
\label{sec:method-embedding-inversion}

The first latent-to-text branch concerns embedding inversion. In this setting,
the input to the inversion method is a fixed-size text embedding, and the output
is a textual reconstruction. The thesis uses this branch as a diagnostic track:
it investigates how embedding-inversion systems behave on semantically fragile
inputs, but it does not rely on this branch alone for the final claim.

The main reproduction target followed a conditional masked diffusion approach
to embedding inversion. The method-level question is whether the reconstructed
sentence preserves the input meaning, especially when the input contains
negation or counterfactual content. Several diagnostics were therefore kept
separate: architecture compatibility, masking behavior, loss behavior, and
checkpoint behavior. This avoids presenting the branch as a single clean
benchmark when some parts are better interpreted as reproduction and debugging
evidence.

\section{SIPIT Hidden-State Inversion}
\label{sec:method-sipit}

SIPIT is used to study hidden-state inversion: the input is a decoder-only
language model hidden state, and the target output is the original prompt token
sequence \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2510-15511}. This branch is important
because it addresses a stronger form of inversion than free-form
reconstruction. If a method exactly recovers the input token sequence, then
semantic properties such as negation are preserved by construction.

The standard SIPIT setting is important as a reference point. In that setting,
the hidden states are generated from an ordinary sequence of vocabulary tokens,
and the recovery target is the same discrete sequence. The extensions below are
harder because they introduce continuous vectors directly in embedding space.
Those vectors may be useful to the model while still not corresponding exactly
to any token in the vocabulary.

The thesis implementation uses SIPIT in three ways. First, it supports the
standard reproduction path on GPT-2-style settings. Second, it exports a logical
dataset derived from the canonical semantic-fidelity rows. Third, it adds a
random-prefix extension. The extension tests what happens when the model input
begins with continuous vectors that are not necessarily token embeddings.

    \centering

        \toprule
        \textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Input to SIPIT} & \textbf{What is recovered} & \textbf{Valid claim} \\

        Standard recovery &

    \caption{SIPIT random-prefix conditions. The two random-prefix modes answer
    different questions and should not be merged into one result.}
    \label{tab:sipit-random-prefix-modes}
\end{table}

The distinction in Table~\ref{tab:sipit-random-prefix-modes} is central. In the
known-prefix control, the random continuous prefix is given as context. SIPIT is
therefore asked only to recover the real prompt after that prefix. In the
full-sequence condition, SIPIT must also map the random continuous prefix to
discrete vocabulary tokens. Since a random vector need not lie exactly on the
token-embedding manifold, exact recovery of the prefix can be impossible or
misleading. The correct interpretation is therefore not simply whether the whole
sequence matches. The method must also ask whether the non-prefix suffix remains
representation-close to the original.

For this reason, the random-prefix branch records suffix diagnostics. For each
failed full-sequence recovery, the recovered non-prefix tokens are compared with
the original non-prefix tokens in two spaces: token-embedding space and
contextual hidden-state space. The same comparison is also made against a
deterministic random-token baseline. If the recovered suffix is much closer
than random in hidden-state space, then the failure is not pure noise even
though exact token recovery failed. If it is close to the random baseline, the
wrong discretized prefix has likely destroyed the useful causal context for the
suffix.

\section{Standalone NLA Activation Verbalization}
\label{sec:method-nla-standalone}

Natural Language Activation (NLA) verbalization is used as a second
latent-to-text view. Instead of trying to recover the exact input string, NLA
maps an activation vector to a natural-language description of what that vector
appears to represent \cite{frasertaliente2026nla}. In this thesis, the relevant
component is the activation verbalizer, not the full autoencoding loop.

The standalone NLA pipeline has four steps. First, the base language model
processes an input example. Second, selected residual-stream activations are
extracted at a compatible layer. Third, the activation verbalizer checkpoint
turns those vectors into text. Fourth, the resulting verbalizations are saved
with enough metadata to recover the source example, selected token, layer, and
token-selection strategy. The NLA project page is used as a software and model
reference for this component.\footnote{\url{https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/nla/index.html}}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white,colframe=black,title=\textbf{Illustrative NLA artifact}]
\small
\textbf{Source text:} a semantically fragile sentence from the canonical dataset.\\
\textbf{Selected token:} one token from the candidate, source, or reference field.\\
\textbf{Layer:} compatible residual-stream layer for the selected base model.\\
\textbf{Verbalization:} a short natural-language description emitted by the AV.\\
\textbf{Caveat:} readability does not prove faithfulness; the verbalization must
be interpreted together with token position, source field, and downstream use.
\end{tcolorbox}

Standalone NLA is useful for validating the extraction path before using the
verbalizations inside GEPA. It also supports the broader semantic-fidelity
question: if an activation is associated with negation or counterfactual
content, does the verbalizer preserve that information or convert it into a
generic plausible description? The answer is empirical and depends on which
tokens are selected, which layer is used, and how the verbalizations are
filtered.

\section{G-EVAL-Style Judge Task}
\label{sec:method-geval-task}

The second part of the method uses G-EVAL-style LLM-as-a-judge evaluation
\cite{DBLP:conf/emnlp/LiuIXWXZ23}. Each example contains an input context, a
candidate output, optional reference or factual information depending on the
dataset, and human scores for one or more evaluation dimensions. The judge model
receives a dimension-specific prompt and must output a score that can be parsed
and compared with the human target.

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small, frame=single]
{
  "context": "...",
  "candidate_output": "...",
  "reference_or_fact": "...",
  "dimension": "engagingness",
  "human_score": 2.67,
  "judge_output": "Score: 3",
  "parsed_score": 3,
  "parsed_score_status": "ok"
}
\end{Verbatim}

The schema above is illustrative, but it captures the method-level contract. The
task is not open-ended generation. The judge must produce a bounded score, and
the score must be parseable. The final objective is agreement with human
annotations, measured by dataset-appropriate correlation metrics in Chapter~4.
During optimization, training and validation examples are used to guide prompt
selection. Held-out final-test examples are used only after the optimized prompt
has been selected.

\section{GEPA Prompt Optimization Loop}
\label{sec:method-gepa-loop}

GEPA is the prompt optimizer used in this thesis \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2507-19457}.
The base judge remains fixed; GEPA changes the judge prompt. A separate
proposer model receives feedback about prompt behavior and proposes revised
instructions. These revised prompts are evaluated, and the best prompt is
selected according to validation behavior.

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small, frame=single]
seed_prompt = initial judge rubric
candidate_pool = {seed_prompt}

for each GEPA iteration:
    evaluate candidate prompts on training/validation examples
    compute agreement metrics against human scores
    collect optional feedback: perplexity, NLA, auxiliary judge
    ask proposer model to write revised prompts
    test revised prompts on validation examples
    keep prompts that improve validation behavior

selected_prompt = best validation prompt
evaluate selected_prompt on held-out final-test examples
\end{Verbatim}

The model roles are deliberately separated. The evaluated judge is the base
Qwen2.5-7B model. The proposer is a stronger Qwen35B model used to propose
prompt edits. The auxiliary judge, when enabled, is also a feedback component
rather than the model whose final scores are reported. This prevents an
important ambiguity: improved final metrics should be attributed to a better
prompt for the base judge, not to replacing the evaluated judge with a stronger
model.

\section{Perplexity Feedback}
\label{sec:method-perplexity-feedback}

Perplexity is used as a response-only signal from the base judge model. Given a
candidate response token sequence $y_1,\ldots,y_T$ and conditioning context $x$,
the average negative log-likelihood is

\[
\mathrm{NLL}(y \mid x) =
-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
\]

and perplexity is

\[
\mathrm{PPL}(y \mid x) = \exp(\mathrm{NLL}(y \mid x)).
\]

Lower perplexity means that the base model assigned higher probability to the
response under the given context. In this thesis, perplexity is not a final
evaluation metric. It is a feedback signal passed to the proposer. For example,
if a wrong score has high perplexity, the proposer may be told that the base
judge was uncertain or that the output did not match the model's expected
format. If a wrong score has low perplexity, the proposer may instead infer that
the prompt consistently encouraged a wrong but confident behavior.

This distinction matters because final evaluation is still based on agreement
with human scores. Perplexity can help explain a model response, but it cannot
replace the target metric.

\section{NLA Feedback For GEPA}
\label{sec:method-nla-feedback}

In the GEPA branch, NLA verbalizations are transformed into feedback for the
prompt proposer. The base judge processes an evaluation example. Selected token
positions are converted into activation vectors. The activation verbalizer turns
those vectors into text. The resulting descriptions are attached to the
reflection data that GEPA gives to the proposer.

Token selection is part of the method. A verbalization of an uninformative token
can produce generic feedback, while too many verbalizations can make the
proposer context long and repetitive. The method therefore records token source,
token position, layer, verbalization text, and deduplication status. Candidate
output tokens, source/context tokens, and reference/fact tokens are treated as
different sources because they can describe different parts of the judgment
problem.

\label{sec:method-aux-judge}

The auxiliary-judge path addresses a practical limitation of raw feedback. GEPA
can consume natural-language feedback, but raw reflection data can become long,
duplicated, or too low-level. The auxiliary judge is used as a compressor: it
receives the example, the human target, the base judge prediction, the error
signal, the base judge rationale, and optional PPL/NLA evidence. It returns a
short prompt-level lesson for the proposer.

\section{Soft-Prompt Training And SIPIT Readout}
\label{sec:method-soft-prompt-readout}

The soft-prompt branch uses the same judge task as the GEPA branch, but changes
the object being optimized. Instead of searching over readable prompt text, the
base judge model is kept frozen and only a small set of continuous
\textbf{virtual prompt tokens} is trained. These vectors are prepended to the
ordinary judge input and optimized through prompt tuning. The branch therefore
answers a different question from GEPA: what can be recovered from a trained
continuous prompt, and whether that recovery contains semantic information
useful for the thesis. Task improvement is used as a sanity check that the
learned vectors encode something relevant before they are passed to SIPIT-style
readout.

The main setting initializes the virtual tokens randomly. Text initialization is
kept only as a control, because it biases any nearest-token interpretation
toward the seed instruction used to initialize the prompt. After training, each
virtual token vector is compared with the model vocabulary embeddings. The
nearest discrete token is selected by L2 distance, while cosine similarity is
stored as a secondary geometric diagnostic. This projection gives a readable
baseline, but it is not by itself a faithful explanation of the learned prompt.

The second readout is SIPIT-style bounded recovery. The learned virtual-token
vectors are treated as continuous targets, and the recovery procedure attempts
to find a sequence of discrete tokens that reproduces the corresponding hidden
states. The verification flag is essential: if the recovered tokens are not
verified, the recovered text must be read only as a diagnostic artifact.

This differs from standard SIPIT. In a standard recovery run, the target hidden
states are generated by real prompt tokens, so exact textual recovery is the
right success criterion. In the soft-prompt readout, the target vectors may be
learned continuous objects with no exact discrete token equivalent. The controls
therefore calibrate the interpretation: one checks that discrete token targets
can be recovered, one checks what happens with a text-initialized prompt under
the same bounded search, and one checks the expected failure mode for arbitrary
continuous vectors.

\section{Artifact And Reproducibility Policy}
\label{sec:method-artifacts}

The method requires preserving enough artifacts to analyze each run without
rerunning expensive jobs. Every long GEPA run should save the seed prompt,
optimized prompt, prompt trajectory, run configuration, split manifest,
per-example predictions, metric outputs, runtime manifest, and logs. Runs with
NLA must additionally save activation-selection metadata and verbalization
artifacts. Runs with auxiliary feedback must save the raw auxiliary inputs and
outputs.

The same principle applies to inversion diagnostics. SIPIT runs should preserve
target token ids, recovered token ids, verification flags, timing, and
condition-specific diagnostics such as prefix and suffix similarity. NLA runs
should preserve activation source fields, token positions, layer indices, and
verbalization text. These artifacts are part of the method because they make the
results interpretable. A scalar score alone cannot explain whether a failure
came from a weak prompt, a missing feedback signal, an uninformative activation,
or a parsing issue.

Soft-prompt runs should additionally save the train configuration, adapter
metadata, validation and final-test metrics, nearest-token diagnostics,
soft-prompt embedding artifacts, and SIPIT readout summaries. Without these
artifacts, it would be impossible to separate a useful continuous prompt from a
faithfully interpretable prompt.

Chapter~4 instantiates this method with concrete datasets, model checkpoints,
metrics, hyperparameters, and execution environment. Chapter~5 then uses the
saved artifacts to evaluate which branches preserve semantic content and which
feedback variants improve, fail to improve, or diagnose GEPA prompt
optimization.
